BACK TO WORKFLOW MODEL 01 • FULL ANALYSIS

1 - Executive Summary

The objective of this analysis was to identify which customers created the most business value and examine whether those patterns changed between Q1 and Q2.

Rather than evaluating all customers as a single group, customers were segmented using two behavioral dimensions: purchase frequency and revenue contribution. These dimensions were selected because they directly reflect customer value creation within the business.

Recency was retained as a supporting behavioral indicator to provide additional context about customer activity, but it was not used as a segmentation criterion.

To ensure fair comparisons across time, separate benchmarks were created for each quarter. This allowed customer segments to be evaluated relative to their own distribution rather than against a fixed threshold that might not accurately represent changing customer behavior.

KEY OUTCOMES

Customer segments were defined using purchase frequency and revenue contribution.

Quarter-specific benchmarks were developed to ensure fair classification across changing distributions.

Customer behavior was compared between Q1 and Q2 to identify shifts in value creation patterns.

The analysis provided a structured framework for tracking how customer value evolves over time.

SECTION 2

Why Segmentation
Was Needed?

Before comparing customer behavior across quarters, one question had to be answered first:
Can all customers really be evaluated using the same standard?

Looking at the data, the answer was clearly no.
A relatively small group of customers generated a large share of revenue and orders, while many others purchased only occasionally. If all customers were analyzed together, those differences would disappear inside the averages.

01

Why Not Analyze All Customers Together?

Overall metrics can tell us what happened. But they rarely explain who actually created the result.

Two quarters may produce similar revenue numbers while being driven by completely different customer groups. So before comparing performance, customers needed to be separated into meaningful segments.

02

Why Focus on Revenue and Order Frequency?

As mentioned earlier, the goal of this analysis was to understand who created business value during each quarter. For that reason, customer value was evaluated using two direct indicators:

  • Revenue generated
  • Number of completed orders

Together, they provide a simple but practical view of customer contribution. Revenue reflects purchasing value, while order frequency reflects purchasing activity.

03

Why Was Recency Used Only as Context?

At first, recency seemed like a natural candidate for segmentation. However, the objective here was not to identify inactive customers or predict churn.

So as mentioned earlier, the question was much simpler: Who created value, and did that change over time?

So, recency was kept as a reference variable rather than a segmentation rule. It provided useful context without affecting how customer groups were classified.

04

Why Use Dynamic Benchmarks?

Customer behavior changed between quarters. Because of that, using a single fixed threshold would create an unfair comparison.

A customer might appear more valuable simply because the benchmark was lower, not because their behavior actually improved. So instead of applying one standard everywhere, benchmarks were recalculated separately for each quarter. This allowed customer groups to be evaluated relative to the distribution they actually belonged to.

3.3 Testing Tukey's IQR Method

To validate the benchmark selection, Tukey's IQR method was also evaluated.

The purpose was not to segment customers using outlier detection, but to test whether statistical boundaries could serve as a stable business benchmark.

TUKEY / IQR VALIDATION TABLE

Although Tukey's method successfully identified statistical outliers, the resulting upper bound changed noticeably between quarters as the distribution shifted.

This made the benchmark sensitive to changes in spread and less suitable for consistent customer segmentation.

The analysis showed that outlier detection is valuable for validation, but not ideal as the primary segmentation benchmark.

3.4 Why Each Quarter Needed Its Own Benchmark

Customer behavior was not identical across Q1 and Q2.

Revenue distributions changed, spending patterns shifted, and statistical boundaries moved accordingly.

Using a single fixed benchmark for both quarters would have created an unfair comparison because customers would be evaluated against a distribution that no longer represented their actual environment.

INSERT REVENUE COMPARISON IMAGE HERE

Recalculating benchmarks for each quarter ensured that customers were always evaluated relative to their own period rather than against historical conditions.

3.5 Frequency Validation Across Quarters

Revenue alone was not enough to validate customer value. Since we made an FM segmentation, purchase frequency was taken into account to see the changes between quarters.

INSERT FREQUENCY VALIDATION IMAGE HERE

The numbers showed a clear change: the VIP threshold increased to 6 orders and the gold tier increased to 4 orders in Q2. This means that customers became more loyal and made more repeat purchases during the second quarter.

The result reinforces our core rule:

"Numbers show the patterns, but business logic drives the decision."

3.6 Final Benchmark Decision

After evaluating averages, percentiles, frequency distributions, and Tukey's method, percentiles were selected as the primary benchmark. They provided:

  • •Flexibility across changing distributions
  • •Consistent comparison between quarters
  • •Clear business interpretation
  • •Better alignment with actual customer behavior

COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) prevented order duplication when joining order items. SUM(revenue) was preserved at item level intentionally — customer value is generated by the complete order basket, not a single line item.

02 - BUSINESS VALIDATION

The SQL execution was correct. But the output exposed a business issue. Customer CUST036 generated over 130,000 EGP in revenue but was classified as Gold — because the fixed FM rule required at least five orders to qualify as VIP. Customers with significantly lower revenue reached VIP simply by meeting the frequency condition.

GAP IDENTIFIED

The SQL was technically correct. The business interpretation was not. Correct syntax does not always produce correct business outcomes. This gap required a calibration decision before moving forward.

02

Model Calibration

Aligning metrics with business strategy — theory meets data.

The misclassification in Step 01 revealed that treating Frequency and Monetary as equal contributors did not reflect how this business actually creates value. Two frameworks were reviewed:

Pareto Principle (80/20) — 80% of value comes from 20% of customers, suggesting revenue should carry more weight than frequency. Weighted Scoring Models — assign different importance to each FM dimension based on business objectives.

Since the objective was to identify customers who create the highest business value and track how that changes over time, revenue was assigned a higher influence than frequency.

This was not an arbitrary choice. It was a business decision — supported by theory, validated against the data, and aligned with the project's objective.

04

The Challenge That Appeared

Classification was solved. But classification alone was never the goal.

From the beginning, the objective was not only to categorize customers — but to understand behavioral patterns over time. Answering these questions required transactional visibility while keeping segmentation stable:

QUESTION 01

How much discount did each segment consume?

QUESTION 02

Did delivery delays affect customer behavior?

QUESTION 03

How did performance change across months?

Three approaches were evaluated — each one failed for a specific reason:

APPROACH
WHY IT FAILED
Adding MONTH(order_date) and status to GROUP BY
The customer was split across months. A VIP customer overall could appear as Gold in a single month — because the FM score recalculated per month, not per quarter.
Multi-layered CTEs
The logic became too complex to explain, maintain, or validate. In a portfolio context, a query no one can follow is not evidence of thinking — it is evidence of confusion.
Raw dates inside the FM GROUP BY
The score calculated at the date level instead of the customer level. The entire FM segmentation logic collapsed.
05

The Solution — Two Tables, One Clean Structure

Instead of forcing all requirements into one query, concerns were separated.

TABLE 01

Customers_Dim

Customer-level data containing the final segmentation result for the entire quarter. One row per customer. The segment label is stable and does not change by month or by query context.

TABLE 02

Sales_Fact

Transaction-level data — order dates, delivery dates, statuses, revenue, and discounts. Connects to Customers_Dim via customer ID for flexible trend and behavioral analysis.

This structure preserved segmentation consistency while enabling flexible analysis across months, statuses, and customer segments — without the query collapsing under its own complexity.

CUSTOMERS_DIM - QUERY

CUSTOMERS_DIM - RESULT

SALES_FACT - QUERY

Transactional detail · Order + delivery + status + discounts

Q2

The same logic and table structure were applied to Q2 using the quarter-specific benchmarks from Section 3. No architectural changes were required. The design proved flexible enough to handle both quarters without modification — demonstrating that the model could adapt to different periods while maintaining consistency.

KEY TAKEAWAY

"This project was never about writing perfect SQL queries. It was about finding the right structure to answer real business questions."

Technical correctness alone does not guarantee meaningful business outcomes. The final solution succeeded because it preserved both analytical accuracy and business meaning. Sometimes the cleanest solution is not the most complex one — but the one that separates concerns clearly and lets each part do what it does best.

SECTION 8

Reflection

Looking back at the journey,
the thinking, and what comes next.

This project was more than building dashboards.
It was about how I think, how I question,
and how I approach a business problem.

01

What Changed

This project started with a simple business question:
Who creates the business value, and does that change over time?

At first, I thought the challenge would be writing SQL queries or building dashboards.
It wasn't.
The real challenge was deciding what deserved to be measured in the first place.

More than once, I stepped back before moving forward. The original RFM model became FM. The scoring logic changed. SQL queries were rewritten. Even after the dashboard was finished, the analysis kept changing because new questions kept appearing.

Every answer created another question.
Every assumption needed evidence.
And every investigation changed the way I looked at the previous result.

That is probably the biggest thing that changed during this project.
I no longer start by asking, "What can I calculate?"
I start by asking, "What business question am I trying to answer?"

02

What I Would Do Differently

If I built this module again, I would spend more time validating the business logic before writing the first query.

The first version of the model was technically correct, but it did not fully reflect how the business creates value. I only realized that after seeing the results and challenging my own assumptions.

A correct query does not always answer the correct business question.

Another thing I would improve is documenting my assumptions earlier. Throughout the project, many insights came from testing ideas, rejecting some of them, and refining others. Writing those assumptions from the beginning would have made the investigation more structured.

03

What This Opened

Although this module answered the original question, it also opened new ones.

The analysis showed operational signals such as delivery delays, cancellations, and returns. These patterns do not prove causation, but they clearly highlight where deeper investigation should begin.

It also reminded me that dashboards are not the end of an analysis. They are the starting point for conversations between analysts, operations, marketing, and business teams.

Looking back at this module, I do not see a collection of charts or SQL queries.
I see a record of decisions, assumptions, revisions, and continuous improvement.

That is what I wanted this portfolio to demonstrate.

Not that I can build dashboards.
But that I know how to think through a business problem, question my own assumptions, and keep improving the answer until the data supports it.
