Section 01

The Question Comes First

Every analysis starts with a business question — not with SQL, dashboards, or tools.

One Question, Many Paths
A single business question can often be answered using several technical approaches. Writing a working query is only one part of the process. The more important decision is choosing the approach that produces an answer that is reliable, explainable, and appropriate for the business context.
What This Model Explores
What happens between receiving a business question and delivering a trustworthy answer. The gap between those two moments is where the real analysis lives.
Business Question
"What is the most reliable way to measure product performance across the available analysis period?"
The business question is simple.
Choosing the right technical path is where the real analysis begins.
Section 02

There Is No Universal Formula

Technical decisions should always follow business priorities — not personal preferences.

The "best" solution depends entirely on the business objective.
A company trying to maximize revenue will evaluate products differently from a company trying to clear inventory. A premium brand will prioritize different metrics than a discount retailer. Analytical models should always begin with the business objective — not with SQL, dashboards, or predefined formulas.
Before choosing any SQL approach or scoring model, one question must be answered:
The Starting Point
"What business goal are we trying to optimize?"
That single question determines everything that follows.
Inventory reduction → quantity sold deserves a higher weight.
Premium brand growth → revenue contribution becomes more important.
Customer loyalty → purchase frequency becomes a stronger indicator.
If the objective changes → the scoring model should change as well.
The model adapts to the business.
The business should never adapt to the model.

Once the business objective became clear, the next challenge was no longer what to measure. It became how to measure it. Several technical approaches could answer the same question — but not all of them were equally maintainable, readable, or scalable. That is where the real technical decision-making begins.

Instead of ranking products immediately, first translate business priorities into measurable criteria — Revenue (40%) · Quantity Sold (40%) · Purchase Frequency (20%). These weights represent business priorities rather than mathematical facts.
Why I Considered It
  • Business priorities become explicit

Approach 02
Rejected
Direct Ranking
Instead of building business scores, rank products directly using their actual performance numbers — simple, fast, no business assumptions required.
Why I Considered It
  • Simple and fast to implement
  • No business assumptions introduced
  • Easy to verify and validate
Why I Rejected It
  • Treats all raw numbers as equally important
  • Cannot express business priorities
  • Two products with very different strategic value may appear similar simply because their raw numbers are close
Approach 03
Selected
Hybrid Evaluation
Combine business evaluation with objective ranking. Products are first evaluated according to business priorities — then the final ranking is generated from those evaluated scores.
Why I Selected It
  • Preserves business thinking without losing objectivity
  • Produces a defensible, explainable ranking
  • Easier to maintain when business priorities evolve
  • Balances analytical rigor with business flexibility
  • Connects business priorities with consistent technical implementation
Final Decision
Hybrid Evaluation — The Most Practical Solution
Rather than allowing raw numbers alone to drive decisions — or relying only on manually weighted scores — the hybrid approach connects business priorities with consistent technical implementation. This makes it the most practical and explainable solution for this project.
Business Alignment
Business priorities remain explicit and adjustable. The model adapts when the objective changes — without rebuilding the entire structure.
Technical Alignment
The SQL implementation follows the business logic directly — readable, maintainable, and scalable as data or requirements evolve.

Model 03 — Section 04

From Business Decision
to Technical Implementation

Turning the Selected Approach into a Practical Solution

In the previous section, the objective was to answer one question:
What is the best way to measure product performance?
After evaluating multiple approaches, I selected a Hybrid Weighted Scoring model — because it evaluates products using three business dimensions instead of relying on a single metric. Revenue reflects business value. Quantity measures demand. Frequency captures customer purchasing behavior.
At first, I thought selecting the right approach would complete the analysis. I was wrong.
Choosing the approach was only half of the solution. The real challenge was transforming that business decision into a technical implementation that is accurate, maintainable, scalable, and easy to explain.
How can this business approach be implemented correctly?
This section answers that question.
SQL Solution

The Implementation

SQL Query Screenshot
3 CTEs · Min-Max Normalization · Weighted Score · ROW_NUMBER Ranking
The SQL implementation follows the same business logic developed in Section 3.
Instead of calculating product performance directly, the query first summarizes each product's business metrics, standardizes them into a comparable scale, and finally applies the agreed business weights to calculate a single Final Score used for ranking.
The structure is intentional — each CTE handles one responsibility, making the logic transparent and auditable at every step.
Why This SQL Design

The Problem Before the Solution

One technical challenge appeared immediately.
The Problem — Incompatible Units
Revenue
Currency (EGP)
Quantity
Units Sold
Frequency
Number of Orders
Since these metrics use completely different scales, combining them directly would produce misleading results. Revenue would dominate the final score simply because its values are much larger — not because it is necessarily more important.
↓
Solution: Min-Max Normalization — Convert every metric into the same range (0–1) while preserving the relative ranking between products. Once every metric is on the same scale, applying business weights becomes meaningful and reflects business priorities rather than differences in measurement units.
-- Min-Max Normalization Formula
Score = (X - MIN(X)) / NULLIF(MAX(X) - MIN(X), 0)

-- Applied to each metric via Window Functions
Revenue_Score  → (Revenue - MIN(Revenue) OVER()) / ...
Quantity_Score  → (Quantity - MIN(Quantity) OVER()) / ...
Frequency_Score → (Frequency - MIN(Frequency) OVER()) / ...
Only after this normalization step were the business weights applied:
0%
Revenue
Commercial value — how much each product generates
0%
Quantity Sold
Actual demand volume in the market
0%
Frequency
Customer purchasing behavior and product stickiness
This ensures the final score represents business value — not numerical magnitude.
Readable
The query is divided into logical CTEs, making each step responsible for one business task. Easy to understand and review without running the full query.
Maintainable
Business rules, weights, or additional metrics can be modified without rewriting the entire query — each transformation is isolated in its own CTE.
Scalable
The same design can be reused for customers, cities, or categories by simply changing the source dataset while preserving the same scoring framework.
Business-Aligned
Every calculation answers a business question. The implementation prioritizes explainability and decision-making instead of technical complexity.

Decision Considerations Before Making Business Decisions
The analysis provides answers — but these three considerations should frame how those answers are used.
01
Business Goals Define the Evaluation Method
There is no universal scoring model. The weights used here (40%-40%-20%) reflect this specific business objective. Different business priorities may require a completely different evaluation approach.
02
Strong Dependency Deserves Attention
A significant share of revenue is generated by a limited number of products and categories. This strengthens short-term performance — but also increases business risk if demand shifts unexpectedly.
03
Discounts Should Be Evaluated in Context
Discounts should never be judged in isolation. They become meaningful only when compared with the revenue and customer demand they help generate.

AI as a
Thinking Partner

Not a replacement — a collaborator in the analytical process.

Throughout this portfolio, every analytical decision was intentionally explained. At this point, one question naturally follows:
Where did AI fit into this process?
Rather than replacing analysis, AI was used as a thinking partner that challenged assumptions, accelerated learning, and improved communication — while leaving every final analytical decision entirely under my responsibility.
AI Role
01
Challenge My Thinking
AI was used to question assumptions, compare alternative approaches, and expose weaknesses before implementation. Instead of accepting the first solution, I deliberately explored different possibilities before choosing the one that best matched the business objective.
02
Validate Decisions
From SQL logic to dashboard design and business interpretation, AI acted as a second reviewer. It helped verify ideas, highlight edge cases, and identify improvements before they became part of the final solution.
03
Accelerate Iteration
Instead of spending hours testing every possible direction manually, AI shortened the feedback loop. This allowed more time for analytical reasoning and business understanding — the parts that require human judgment.
04
Improve Communication
Building a solution is only part of the job. Explaining why that solution exists is equally important. AI helped refine explanations and organize technical concepts into business-focused narratives — without changing the analytical reasoning behind them.
AI Contributed
  • Questioning assumptions and suggesting alternatives
  • Reviewing SQL logic and flagging edge cases
  • Shortening the iteration cycle
  • Refining written explanations and communication
  • Offering second opinions on design decisions
Human Decided
  • Selecting the analytical approach and business framework
  • Defining the scoring weights and evaluation criteria
  • Interpreting what the data actually means
  • Making every final decision from SQL to dashboard design
  • Taking responsibility for the conclusions presented
Section 05 — Closing
"AI was never the objective of this portfolio — it was one of the tools used throughout the journey."
The real objective was to demonstrate a structured analytical process: understanding the business problem, evaluating alternative approaches, validating decisions, implementing the solution, and communicating the results clearly.

Every final decision — from selecting the analytical approach to designing the SQL logic, building the dashboards, and interpreting the insights — remained a deliberate human decision. AI served as a partner that strengthened the thinking rather than replacing it.

Business Thinking Before Technical Execution
"The hardest part was not the RFM framework or the SQL. It was stepping back before writing a single query and asking: what threshold would actually make this segmentation fair and defensible?"
That question led to a benchmark built from the data itself — not from assumptions. It led to a weighted scoring model when equal weights produced misleading results. The model started as a segmentation project. It became a lesson in defining what value means before measuring it.
The most important analytical decisions happen before the first line of code.

Confidence Before Conclusions
"The hardest part was not finding the errors. It was deciding what each error actually meant."
A NULL value is not always missing data. A duplicate is not always a mistake. An outlier is not always noise. Every validation step required a business judgment — not just a technical fix — because the goal was never to clean the data. The goal was to trust it.
Without this model, every conclusion in Models 01 and 03 would have been built on unvalidated ground.
Reliable insights depend on reliable foundations — not on attractive dashboards.

Decisions Over Answers
"The hardest part was accepting that there is no universal formula."
Multiple approaches could answer the same business question. The real challenge was evaluating each one against business priorities — rejecting what was technically correct but businessly misleading, and choosing the approach that could be explained, defended, and adapted when priorities change.
