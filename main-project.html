02 • WHY I BUILT MY OWN DATASET

Why I Built
My Own Dataset

Building the data became part of the learning process itself.

This project was born from the need for a realistic analytical environment. I wanted data that behaves more like a real business system — full of relationships, imperfections, operational patterns, and decisions made by humans.

Instead of relying only on public datasets, I built one from scratch to simulate how data is created, processed, and affected by real-world business behavior.

SYSTEM NOTE

This portfolio was built to explore the reasoning behind decisions, not only the tools behind dashboards.

01

Beyond Ready-Made Data

Public datasets were a valuable starting point, but many of them were either too clean, too isolated, or disconnected from realistic business behavior. I was not looking only for tables to query, but for a system that behaves like the real world.

02

Simulating Business Reality

Real-world problems are created by human decisions, operational workflows, system behavior, and business processes behind the data. I intentionally introduced delays, returns, inconsistencies, and KPI distortions to simulate reality.

03

Why E-Commerce?

E-Commerce provides a rich and highly connected environment involving customers, orders, products, categories, shipping, cities, and purchasing patterns. It also opens the door to supply chain analysis on a broader level.

04

Learning Through Building

Building the environment became part of the learning process. Every broken relationship, inconsistent value, delayed order, or distorted KPI created a new question that needed to be understood before it could be solved.

03 • DATA ARCHITECTURE & BUSINESS COMPLEXITY

Dataset Architecture
& Business Complexity

Structured as a Connected System

After explaining why I built my own dataset in Section 2, this section now shows exactly how the data was structured.

1

DATASET ARCHITECTURE

The dataset was designed as a connected business environment rather than a single flat table.

It included customers, orders, order items, products, categories, shipping behavior, and cities connected through relational modeling principles to better simulate how transactional systems behave in real analytical environments.

The goal was not only to store data, but to preserve the relationships and business context behind the numbers.

2

WHY MULTIPLE RELATED TABLES?

Instead of simplifying everything into one dataset, the project was intentionally built using multiple related tables to reflect how data is usually generated across operational systems.

This structure made it possible to work with joins, aggregations, relationship behavior, and analytical consistency in a more realistic way while also exposing common issues such as duplication, distorted metrics, and reporting challenges.

3

STAR SCHEMA DESIGN

The schema follows a star design where Orders and Order Items serve as fact-like tables, connected to surrounding dimension tables including Customers, Products, Categories, and Shipping.

4

BUSINESS SCENARIO SIMULATION

The project also included business-driven scenarios rather than technical problems only.

Discount behavior changed across specific periods, shipment delays affected operational patterns, and some KPIs were intentionally distorted through returns and cancelled orders to create situations where analytical decisions required deeper investigation before reaching conclusions.

The presence of these scenarios was built into the architecture itself before any cleaning or analysis began.

5

TRANSITION TO SECTION 4

After establishing the data architecture and relationships between tables, the natural next step was to clean and validate the data. This will be covered in detail in Section 4.

04

DATA QUALITY
& VALIDATION

From Raw Data to Trusted Data

In Section 3, we saw how the data was structured and how the tables were related. Now in Section 4, we will see how the data was cleaned and validated before any business analysis began.

Real business data is rarely clean from the start. Since this dataset was built manually to simulate a realistic e-commerce environment, different types of data quality issues were intentionally introduced.

Before any analysis could begin, the dataset had to be reviewed, cleaned, and validated to ensure that every metric produced later could be trusted.

The following four categories represent the primary data quality challenges that were identified and resolved during the validation process.

01

ORDER ID VALIDATION

Missing & Duplicate IDs

The Challenge

Some Order IDs were missing (NULL) and some orders were duplicated (e.g., ORD009 appearing multiple times).

The Action

Missing IDs were investigated and filled when possible, while duplicate records were removed to ensure each order remains completely unique.

BEFORE
AFTER

Result: Every order has a valid and unique Order ID.

02

DATE STANDARDIZATION

Mixed Formats & Serial Numbers

The Challenge

Date fields appeared in different formats across tables (short dates, serial numbers, text formats).

The Action

All dates were converted to a single standard format (YYYY-MM-DD) and validated to ensure logical consistency across all date fields.

BEFORE
AFTER

Result: All dates are in standard format and follow logical consistency.

03

FORMATTING CONSISTENCY

Status & Payment Method

The Challenge

Text fields contained inconsistent capitalization, extra spaces, and special characters that negatively affected joins, grouping, and reporting.

The Action

Text was cleaned by removing extra spaces and standardizing casing to improve overall consistency without losing the original business context.

BEFORE
AFTER

Result: All text values are consistent and clean for accurate reporting.

04

HELPER COLUMN & DERIVED FIELD

Creating Shipping Cost

The Challenge

Shipping cost was not natively available as a single column in any source table.

The Action

It was derived by calculating the difference between total amount and product cost. A helper column was intentionally created instead of modifying existing columns to preserve complete data transparency and traceability.

BEFORE
AFTER

Result: Shipping cost is now available as a derived field with full traceability.

SECTION 05

What The Data
Started Revealing

Moving from analysis to understanding

After building the dataset, designing the structure, and validating the data, the next step was no longer improving the data itself. The focus shifted toward understanding what the data was actually showing. Rather than treating dashboards as final outputs, they became a way to compare perspectives, question assumptions, and connect numbers with business context.

BLOCK 01

Macro View

Seeing The Bigger Picture

The first step was stepping back and looking at the business as a whole. Instead of focusing on individual metrics, this view was designed to understand overall movement across sales, profitability, operational performance, customer activity, and changes over time.

The objective was not to answer every question here. It was to build context and identify where deeper investigation might be needed. That broader view naturally led to a more focused level of analysis.

BLOCK 02

Micro View

Exploring What Deserved Attention

After identifying broader patterns, the next step was narrowing the scope and examining areas that appeared more meaningful. This level focused less on overall performance and more on comparison, behavior, and changing conditions across different dimensions.

Some observations appeared connected at first glance, but patterns alone were not enough to support conclusions. This stage required moving beyond surface interpretation and looking more carefully at what the numbers might actually represent.

BLOCK 03

Details View

Looking Beyond The Summary

Once broader patterns became visible, deeper exploration helped move beyond aggregated results and investigate the factors behind them. At this stage, the objective was not to collect more numbers.

It was to understand what influenced those numbers and whether the initial assumptions remained valid after closer examination. Some findings became clearer. Others became more complex. Those observations eventually shaped the final business understanding.

BLOCK 04

Key Insights

Connecting The Findings

The final step was bringing observations together and turning separate findings into a more connected understanding. Not every observation became an insight immediately. Some findings later became starting points for more focused analytical exploration, where questions, assumptions, and emerging patterns were examined in greater depth before reaching more meaningful conclusions.

Several of those directions later evolved into more structured analytical work and deeper investigation. The project did not end with dashboards. It changed how I approach questions, validate assumptions, and understand business performance.

SECTION 06

Project Outcomes
& What I Learned

The real outcome was not the dashboard. It was the way I think today.

01

DEFINITIONS BEFORE CALCULATIONS

When I started this project, I thought the difficult part would be writing complex SQL queries, building advanced Power BI visuals, or learning new analytical techniques. I was wrong.

The deeper I got into the project, the more I realized that most analytical problems are not technical problems. They are definition problems. One of the biggest turning points came when I discovered that several KPIs I had calculated correctly were still producing misleading business conclusions. The queries were accurate. The formulas were accurate. The dashboard looked accurate. Yet the story was wrong because I had not fully questioned what those metrics were supposed to represent in the first place.

Instead of starting with calculations, I learned to start with definitions. Instead of asking how to calculate a KPI, I learned to ask what that KPI actually means from a business perspective.

02

DATA CHALLENGED MY ASSUMPTIONS

What surprised me most was how often the data challenged my assumptions. There were multiple occasions where I expected one conclusion, only to discover that the underlying business logic pointed somewhere completely different.

Even simple questions such as sales, revenue, orders, or profit became more complex once returns, cancellations, discounts, delivery delays, and operational constraints were taken into account.

03

ANALYTICS IS MORE THAN DASHBOARDS

As the project evolved, so did my understanding of analytics. The dashboard gradually became the least interesting part of the work.

Most of the value came from investigating anomalies, validating calculations, testing assumptions, and understanding how operational processes influence business outcomes.

The Macro, Micro, and Details layers of the dashboard reflect that evolution in thinking. The first layer focuses on understanding what happened. The second investigates where the results came from. The third explores the operational drivers behind those results.

04

SELF-LEARNING SHAPED MY GROWTH

Self-learning played a significant role in that process. It provided complete freedom to explore different ideas, approaches, and techniques, but it also meant there was no predefined path to follow.

Many of the most valuable lessons came from mistakes, rework, and assumptions that did not survive contact with the data. In hindsight, those moments contributed more to my growth than any successful dashboard build.

05

HOW I APPROACH ANALYTICS TODAY

Today, I approach analytics very differently than I did 13 months ago.

I am less interested in producing answers quickly and more interested in understanding whether those answers deserve to be trusted. I spend more time questioning assumptions, validating business logic, and evaluating data quality before drawing conclusions.

06

THE REAL OUTCOME

This project started as an attempt to build a dashboard. It became an exercise in analytical thinking.

The dashboard is simply the final artifact. The real outcome was learning how to navigate uncertainty, challenge assumptions, and develop confidence in the conclusions that support business decisions.

This project did not end here.

It became the foundation for a deeper analytical workflow and a structured way of solving business problems.
